## Abstract

**Background:** Working memory (WM) training has been proposed as a strategy to mitigate age-related cognitive decline, yet evidence for its effectiveness in producing durable, generalizable gains in healthy older adults remains inconclusive.

**Objective:** To systematically review randomized and quasi-randomized controlled trials of WM training in healthy older adults (≥60 years), examining training parameters, transfer effects, and potential moderating factors.

**Methods:** We searched four databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CNKI) from inception to February 2025. Studies were included if they: (1) enrolled healthy older adults aged ≥60 years without neurological or psychiatric diagnoses; (2) employed a WM-targeted cognitive training program with ≥2 sessions; (3) measured at least one cognitive outcome; and (4) used RCT, quasi-RCT, crossover, or single-group pre–post design. Narrative synthesis was conducted due to high methodological heterogeneity.

**Results:** Across 56 included studies, near transfer was assessed in 46 studies (82%) and far transfer in 43 studies (77%). Overall, transfer effects were limited and highly variable: only 9 studies (16%) reported consistent positive transfer across all outcomes, 12 studies (21%) showed mixed findings, and 35 studies (63%) found no significant between-group differences on any transfer measure. Among studies assessing near transfer, 30 of 46 (65%) yielded null overall conclusions, indicating that even proximal generalization is not reliably achieved under controlled conditions. These patterns underscore the importance of identifying moderating factors that distinguish studies with and without transfer effects.

**Conclusions:** WM training produces reliable improvements on trained tasks in healthy older adults, but evidence for meaningful transfer to untrained tasks remains limited, particularly when active control comparisons are used. Future research should prioritize theoretically grounded task selection, pre-registration, and long-term follow-up.

**Keywords:** working memory training, cognitive training, older adults, transfer effects, systematic review

## 1. Introduction

Working memory (WM)—the capacity to temporarily maintain and manipulate information in the service of ongoing cognitive tasks—undergoes substantial decline across the adult lifespan (Baddeley, 2000; Park & Festini, 2017). Given that WM capacity is associated with higher-order cognition, including fluid intelligence, language comprehension, and everyday problem-solving (Conway et al., 2003; Süß et al., 2002), its age-related deterioration has motivated extensive interest in cognitive training as a potential intervention.

Over the past two decades, computerized WM training programs—particularly adaptive n-back paradigms—have been widely studied in younger adults (Jaeggi et al., 2008; Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016). The central question driving this research is whether improvements gained through repeated practice on WM tasks *transfer* to untrained cognitive domains—a phenomenon termed "far transfer." Near transfer, defined as generalization to untrained tasks that share high cognitive process overlap with the training task, has been more consistently observed; evidence for far transfer to measures of fluid intelligence, attentional control, or everyday functioning has been considerably more mixed (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Shipstead et al., 2012).

In older adults, the question of WM training transfer is particularly pertinent, given the potential to attenuate cognitive aging trajectories. Several theoretical frameworks predict age-related differences in training responsiveness. The Compensation-Related Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis (CRUNCH; Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008) proposes that older adults over-recruit neural resources under moderate cognitive load, potentially limiting further plasticity from training. Conversely, the *cognitive reserve* hypothesis suggests that individuals with greater reserve may show greater training-induced plasticity (Stern, 2009). The *magnification hypothesis* predicts larger training benefits for those with lower baseline performance, owing to greater room for improvement (Lindenberger et al., 2008).

Despite a growing literature, reviews of WM training in older adults have reached divergent conclusions. Lampit et al. (2014) reported modest positive effects of computerized cognitive training across multiple cognitive domains. However, more rigorous meta-analyses by Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016) and Sala & Gobet (2019) found near-zero effects when active control comparisons were used and criterion validity was considered. More recent individual studies continue to yield heterogeneous findings, with variation attributable to differences in training design, participant characteristics, control group type, and outcome measurement.

Several methodological features have been proposed as moderators of training efficacy: (1) training *dose* (number of sessions, total hours), with longer programs hypothesized to produce larger effects; (2) training *adaptivity*, which adjusts difficulty to individual performance and may promote deeper engagement; (3) the presence of an *active control group*, which controls for non-specific factors such as social contact, motivation, and test familiarity; (4) baseline WM capacity, with low-performers possibly benefiting more; and (5) the *cognitive process overlap* between training and transfer tasks, conceptualized within Barnett and Ceci's (2002) taxonomy of transfer distance.

The present systematic review aims to (1) provide a comprehensive, up-to-date synthesis of WM training effects in healthy older adults aged ≥60 years; (2) characterize the range of training parameters employed across studies; and (3) examine evidence for potential moderators of near- and far-transfer outcomes using a narrative synthesis approach. Given demonstrated high methodological heterogeneity, we did not conduct a meta-analysis; instead, we apply vote-counting with evidence-strength classifications to summarize patterns across studies.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Registration and Protocol

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). This review was not pre-registered. A systematic search and screening protocol was followed in accordance with PRISMA 2020 reporting standards.

### 2.2 Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria, framed within the PICOS framework:

**Population (P):** Healthy older adults with mean age ≥60 years. Studies were excluded if they enrolled participants with diagnosed neurological conditions (e.g., Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, mild cognitive impairment [MCI]), acquired brain injury, major psychiatric disorders, or if the sample mean age was <60 years.

**Intervention (I):** A cognitive training program in which working memory was the primary target. The training must have comprised ≥2 sessions. Programs combining WM training with non-cognitive co-interventions (e.g., transcranial direct current stimulation [tDCS], physical exercise, or pharmacological agents) were included, provided that a WM training component was present and clearly described.

**Comparator (C):** Any comparison condition was acceptable, including: active control groups (receiving an alternative cognitive activity); passive control groups (no-contact or waitlist); or single-group pre–post designs (no comparator). Studies with active controls were analyzed separately from those with passive controls or single-group designs where relevant.

**Outcomes (O):** At least one cognitive outcome must have been assessed. Primary outcomes were: (a) near-transfer effects—performance on untrained WM or closely related tasks (same modality, same cognitive process); (b) far-transfer effects—performance on tasks requiring different cognitive processes (e.g., fluid intelligence, attention, episodic memory, processing speed, everyday functioning). Studies measuring only criterion training task performance without any transfer outcome were excluded.

**Study design (S):** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-randomized controlled trials, crossover designs, and single-group pre–post designs were all eligible. Review articles, protocols, editorials, commentaries, case reports, and conference abstracts without full-text data were excluded.

**Language:** English only.

### 2.3 Search Strategy

We conducted systematic searches in four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO (via EBSCOhost), Web of Science Core Collection, and CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure). The search was conducted in [month/year; to be confirmed]. No publication date restriction was applied.

The search strategy combined terms related to (1) working memory training or cognitive training, (2) older adults or aging, and (3) cognitive outcomes or transfer. An illustrative search string for PubMed was:

> ("working memory training" OR "cognitive training" OR "brain training" OR "n-back") AND ("older adults" OR "elderly" OR "aging" OR "older people") AND ("transfer" OR "cognitive function" OR "cognitive performance")

Full database-specific search strings are available in Supplementary Materials.

### 2.4 Study Selection

Search results from all four databases were combined and deduplicated using a Python-based pipeline (fitz and openpyxl libraries), resulting in a deduplicated pool of 4,168 records. Title and abstract screening was conducted in two stages:

**Stage 1 (Automated pre-screening):** Keyword-based filtering retained records containing terms related to WM training and older adults (1,789 records passed to Stage 2; 2,379 excluded).

**Stage 2 (Title/abstract review):** Kimi (a large language model assistant) conducted initial screening of all 1,789 records against PICOS criteria, followed by independent human confirmation of all borderline decisions. Stage 2 excluded 1,344 records, retaining 445 for full-text review.

**Stage 3 (Full-text eligibility):** Full-text PDFs were obtained for all 445 records. Full-text screening against all inclusion and exclusion criteria was conducted, resulting in 56 studies meeting all eligibility criteria. Reasons for exclusion of the 15 most recently reviewed ineligible full-texts were: population not meeting age criteria (*k* = 12), intervention not meeting WM-training criteria (*k* = 2), and design criteria not met (*k* = 1). A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

### 2.5 Data Extraction

Data were extracted from full-text PDFs using a standardized extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel (57 fields). Extraction was conducted through a combination of automated PDF text parsing (Python/PyMuPDF) and manual verification. The following information was extracted for each study:

- *Bibliographic information:* First author, year, country of study, journal/publication status
- *Sample characteristics:* Total *N*, training group *N*, control group *N*, mean age, age SD, proportion female, education (years), cognitive screening tool and score
- *Training parameters:* Training type (n-back, span-based, other), adaptive vs. fixed difficulty, total sessions, session duration (minutes), training weeks, training frequency (sessions/week), training platform/software, setting (laboratory, home-based, mixed), combined intervention (tDCS, TMS, exercise, medication)
- *Study design:* RCT, quasi-RCT, crossover, single-group pre–post; control group type (active vs. passive)
- *Outcomes:* Near-transfer assessed (yes/no), near-transfer outcome variables; far-transfer assessed (yes/no), far-transfer outcome variables and domains; follow-up assessment (yes/no), follow-up time point (months)
- *Results:* Overall between-group conclusion on transfer outcomes (Positive/Null/Mixed), effect size reported (yes/no), Cohen's *d* if available
- *Neuroimaging:* Whether neuroimaging outcomes were assessed (yes/no), modality (fMRI, EEG, ERP, other), main findings
- *Moderator-related:* Baseline WM level (High/Low/not reported); whether explicit moderator effect tests were conducted; direction of significant moderator effects
- *Risk of bias:* See Section 2.6

The "Overall Conclusion" field was coded based on between-group differences on transfer outcomes exclusively. Improvements on the trained criterion tasks, which are expected through practice regardless of training mechanism, were not considered in this coding.

When studies reported multiple experiments or training conditions, data were extracted for the primary WM training condition. For studies comparing dose or schedule conditions without a control group (e.g., Booth et al., 2023), the study was included but dose-specific conclusions were not extracted.

### 2.6 Quality Assessment

Risk of bias (RoB) for RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2; Sterne et al., 2019), which evaluates five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from the intended intervention; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in selection of the reported result. For non-randomized studies (quasi-RCTs and single-group pre–post designs), the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was applied (Sterne et al., 2016). Each study was assigned an overall RoB rating of Low, Some Concerns, or High.

Risk of bias assessment revealed that the majority of included RCTs (*k* = 42, 82%) were rated as "Some Concerns" under RoB 2.0, primarily due to inadequate reporting of randomization procedures, near-universal absence of assessor blinding, and lack of pre-registration. Eight studies (16%) received an overall "Low" risk rating (Tays et al., 2015; Guye et al., 2017; Teixeira-Santos et al., 2022; Verty et al., 2024; Booth et al., 2023; Borella et al., 2025; Pergher et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2026). One RCT (McAvinue et al., 2013) was rated "High" due to a 31% attrition rate with no intention-to-treat analysis reported. Among the five non-randomized studies assessed with ROBINS-I, one crossover design was rated "Moderate" (Šimko et al., 2021), three quasi-RCTs were rated "Serious," and one single-group pre–post study (Günther et al., 2003) received a "Critical" rating due to the complete absence of a control condition. Full RoB ratings are presented in Table 2.

### 2.7 Data Synthesis

Due to the high degree of methodological heterogeneity among included studies—encompassing variability in training tasks, outcome measures, control group types, and participant characteristics—quantitative meta-analysis was not appropriate (Borenstein et al., 2009; Higgins et al., 2019). Instead, we adopted a narrative synthesis approach guided by the Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines (Campbell et al., 2020).

For each study, the direction and significance of primary transfer outcomes were classified as: *Positive* (statistically significant between-group advantage for the training group on ≥1 transfer domain with no significant reverse effects); *Null* (no statistically significant between-group difference on any transfer outcome); or *Mixed* (significant positive effects on some outcomes alongside null effects on others).

Evidence strength for each moderator variable was classified using vote-counting criteria: *Consistent* (>75% of studies within a subgroup showing the same direction); *Moderate* (50–75%); *Mixed* (<50%); *Insufficient* (<3 studies).

One included study (Günther et al., 2003; Seq 35) employed a single-group pre–post design without any control condition. This study was assigned a "Critical" overall ROBINS-I rating. Its "Positive" conclusion reflects pre–post improvement in the absence of a comparator and cannot be attributed to training over and above spontaneous variation or test-retest effects. This study is retained in the synthesis for completeness and to maintain transparency about the full evidence base, but its contribution to subgroup patterns is interpreted with corresponding caution.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Study Selection and Overview

The database searches identified 4,168 unique records after deduplication. Following three-stage screening, 56 studies published between 2003 and 2026 met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (see Figure 1 for PRISMA flow diagram).

The 56 included studies were conducted across 17 countries, with the highest concentrations in the United States (*k* = 19, 34%), Italy (*k* = 8, 14%), Germany (*k* = 8, 14%), and Switzerland (*k* = 4, 7%). Publication years ranged from 2003 to 2026, with notable increases from 2013 onward; the most productive years were 2020 (*k* = 7), 2014 (*k* = 6), and 2017 (*k* = 6) (see Figure 2).

A total of 53 studies reported sufficient data to estimate total sample size. Across these, combined sample sizes ranged from *N* = 9 (Vesterinen et al., 2018) to *N* = 487 (Zelinski et al., 2014), with a median of 54 participants. Mean participant age across studies ranged from 61.9 years (Pergher et al., 2020) to 90.0 years (Goghari et al., 2017), with a median mean age of 69.7 years. A total of 48 studies (86%) included participants in the "young-old" category (mean age ≤75 years); 8 studies (14%) focused on "old-old" samples (mean age >75 years).

The majority of studies employed n-back training paradigms (*k* = 44, 79%), followed by span-based tasks (*k* = 5, 9%), and composite or alternative WM programs (*k* = 7, 12%). Nearly all studies used adaptive training protocols (*k* = 45, 80%). Common training platforms included Cogmed, BrainStim, and custom research programs. Training settings were predominantly laboratory-based (*k* = 39, 70%), with a minority employing home-based (*k* = 10, 18%) or mixed formats (*k* = 7, 12%).

Fifty-one studies (91%) were randomized controlled trials (RCTs); 3 (5%) were quasi-RCTs; 1 (2%) used a crossover design; and 1 (2%) was a single-group pre–post design. Forty-six studies (82%) included an active control group; 10 studies (18%) used passive controls or no-contact designs.

Fourteen studies (25%) combined WM training with a co-intervention, most commonly transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS; *k* = 10 studies with some form of brain stimulation), physical exercise (*k* = 11), or pharmacological agents (*k* = 7). Fifteen studies (27%) implemented pure WM training without any co-intervention.

Risk of bias assessment indicated that 8 of 51 RCTs (16%) were rated Low overall, 42 (82%) received Some Concerns ratings, and 1 (McAvinue et al., 2013) was rated High due to high attrition without ITT analysis. The predominant sources of bias were inadequate randomization reporting, absence of assessor blinding, and lack of pre-registration—limitations that are characteristic of the cognitive training literature broadly (see Table 2).

### 3.2 Near Transfer Effects

*Note: The "overall conclusion" classification reflects the aggregate pattern across all reported transfer outcomes (near and far combined). A near-transfer-specific conclusion field was not coded; readers should interpret near transfer findings in this section accordingly.*

Near transfer was assessed in 46 of 56 included studies (82%), making it the most commonly evaluated outcome domain. Near transfer measures included untrained working memory variants such as digit span, Corsi block tasks, letter-number sequencing, and alternative n-back versions—tasks sharing high cognitive process overlap with the training paradigm (Barnett & Ceci, 2002).

Despite the theoretical expectation that near transfer should be the most reliably obtained benefit of WM training, findings were notably inconsistent. Of the 46 studies that included near transfer assessment, 9 (20%) reported significant training-related improvements relative to control conditions across all reported outcomes, 7 (15%) showed mixed findings (i.e., significant effects on some but not all near transfer measures), and 30 (65%) found no significant between-group differences.

It should be noted that the overall conclusion field was coded based solely on between-group differences on transfer outcomes; improvements on criterion training tasks were not included in this judgment. Studies using passive control conditions (*k* = 10 within the near-transfer subsample) tended to report more favorable near transfer outcomes (Positive: 38%; Null: 25%; Mixed: 38%) compared to those using active controls (*k* = 38; Positive: 16%; Null: 74%; Mixed: 11%), consistent with the interpretation that non-specific motivational and engagement factors contribute to apparent training gains (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016).

Among studies reporting effect sizes (*k* = 10 with numeric *d* values), Cohen's *d* for transfer outcomes ranged from 0.22 to 1.73, with a median of approximately 0.95. The median effect size should be interpreted with caution: it is based on only 10 of the 56 included studies that reported quantitative effect size statistics; the remaining 46 did not provide sufficient information for effect size computation. Furthermore, effect sizes were derived from studies with varying control conditions, and those using passive controls likely inflate this estimate. These caveats underscore the need to identify moderating factors that can account for outcome variability across studies.

The variability in near transfer outcomes across studies suggests that factors beyond training exposure per se—including participant characteristics, control group type, and training design features—play a meaningful moderating role (see Section 3.4).

### 3.3 Far Transfer Effects

> *Note. Outcome domains below were classified by the reviewer based on reported transfer measures, cross-referenced with the far-transfer domain classification field in the extraction table. Where the domain field was incomplete, classification followed the variable descriptions in the far-transfer outcome variable field.*

Far transfer was assessed in 43 of 56 included studies (77%). Far transfer measures spanned multiple cognitive domains, including fluid intelligence, executive function (inhibitory control, task-switching, attention, processing speed), episodic memory, and everyday functioning. Consistent with prior meta-analytic evidence (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016; Sala & Gobet, 2019), far transfer effects were limited overall: 29 of 43 studies (67%) with far transfer assessment reported null overall conclusions, 9 (21%) reported positive outcomes, and 5 (12%) reported mixed results.

#### 3.3.1 Fluid Intelligence

Fluid intelligence was assessed as a far-transfer outcome in 15 studies (35% of far-transfer studies), most commonly using Raven's Progressive Matrices or the Cattell Culture-Fair Test. Positive overall conclusions were rare in this domain: only 2 studies—Teixeira-Santos et al. (2022), who combined WM training with tDCS, and Matysiak et al. (2019), who used an extensive 25-session adaptive program with exercise—reported significant improvements on fluid intelligence measures relative to control. One study (Borella et al., 2017) reported mixed findings. The remaining 12 studies found no significant fluid intelligence gains. This pattern converges with meta-analytic conclusions (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016) that transfer to fluid intelligence is not reliably produced by WM training in older adults.

#### 3.3.2 Executive Function, Attention, and Processing Speed

Executive function and related measures (inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, attention, processing speed) represented the most frequently assessed far-transfer domain, appearing in 31 studies (72% of far-transfer studies). Of these, 7 studies (23%) reported positive conclusions, 4 (13%) reported mixed outcomes, and 20 (65%) found null effects. Positive findings were reported by Salminen et al. (2016; visuospatial skills and spatial n-back), Tays et al. (2015; Stroop inhibition), Matysiak et al. (2019; memory updating, inhibition, attention shifting), Verty et al. (2024; processing speed and attention), Günther et al. (2003; Trail Making Test), Shatil et al. (2014; planning and problem solving), and Nguyen et al. (2026; global executive function). The preponderance of null effects in this domain—even for measures such as inhibition that share moderate process overlap with n-back training—suggests that executive skill improvements do not reliably generalize from WM training.

#### 3.3.3 Episodic Memory

Episodic memory was assessed in 13 studies (30% of far-transfer studies). Positive outcomes were observed in 4 studies, mixed in 2, and null in 7. Studies reporting positive or mixed episodic memory effects include Matysiak et al. (2019), Verty et al. (2024), Nguyen et al. (2026), and Buschkuehl et al. (2008; mixed, visual long-term memory). Given the theoretical distance between WM updating and episodic encoding processes, these positive findings may reflect shared attentional resource demand or strategic learning generalization, though evidence remains insufficient to draw firm conclusions (*k* = 4 positive studies).

#### 3.3.4 Everyday Functioning and Quality of Life

Everyday functioning, quality of life, and related outcomes were assessed in 9 studies. Only 2 reported positive overall conclusions: Stephens et al. (2016), which used ecologically valid fNIRS tasks and IADL measures, and Nguyen et al. (2026), which measured prospective memory and well-being. Seven studies (78%) found null effects, including studies explicitly designed to target functional outcomes (e.g., Borella et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2018; McAvinue et al., 2013; Goghari et al., 2018; Zinke et al., 2014). Given the substantial process distance between laboratory WM training and everyday cognitive demands, the limited transfer to this domain is consistent with the identical elements framework (Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901).

#### 3.3.5 Overall Pattern

Across all far transfer domains, the pattern of findings indicates that transfer is neither uniform nor inevitable. Positive far transfer was most likely to be reported in studies using combined interventions (e.g., WM + tDCS) or extended training programs, though sample sizes within these subgroups are small. The degree to which training-induced improvements generalize appears to depend on the cognitive process overlap between the training task and the transfer measure—a dimension examined in detail in Section 3.4.4.

### 3.4 Moderators of Transfer Effects

#### 3.4.1 Baseline Working Memory Ability

Eight of the 56 included studies formally tested baseline WM capacity as a moderator of training-induced transfer. Results were inconsistent, with evidence supporting both compensatory and magnification accounts.

Three studies found evidence consistent with the **magnification hypothesis**—that individuals with lower baseline WM benefit more from training. Zinke et al. (2014; seq25) found that old-old participants with lower initial WM scores showed significantly greater training gains and near-transfer improvements than higher-performing peers. Borella et al. (2017; seq53) found a similar pattern: participants with lower baseline WM demonstrated larger improvements on the training task and some transfer measures relative to high-baseline participants, though this did not translate into a consistent overall positive conclusion. Brambilla et al. (2021; seq54) similarly found greater near-transfer benefit among lower-baseline participants in a tDCS-augmented WM training context.

Three studies found **no significant moderation** by baseline WM: Matysiak et al. (2019; seq13), Cantarella et al. (2017; seq30), and Stephens et al. (2017; seq43) all reported null baseline-moderation effects, with transfer outcomes not systematically differing across WM ability levels at baseline.

Two studies reported exploratory findings without formal moderation tests: Goghari et al. (2017; seq49) and Zelinski et al. (2014; seq57) extracted baseline performance as a covariate but did not report moderator interaction terms.

Taken together, the evidence regarding baseline WM ability as a moderator remains inconclusive (*mixed evidence*). The divergence between compensation and magnification effects may reflect a task difficulty interaction: adaptive training protocols that calibrate difficulty to individual performance may disproportionately benefit lower-ability individuals, whereas fixed-difficulty protocols may favor those with sufficient cognitive resources to engage effectively with the demands of training.

#### 3.4.2 Age Group

Across the 56 studies, 42 (75%) enrolled samples classified as young-old (mean age ≤75 years), while 14 (25%) enrolled old-old samples (mean age >75 years). Positive overall transfer conclusions were observed in 11 of 42 young-old studies (26%) and 2 of 14 old-old studies (14%). Null conclusions were more prevalent in the old-old group (71%) compared to young-old (60%), though absolute differences were modest.

Thirteen studies (23%) included formal age subgroup analyses or reported age as a moderator. Among these, the majority found no significant within-sample age moderation of transfer outcomes. Heinzel et al. (2014; seq18) found no age-moderation in a sample spanning 60–79 years. Jaeggi et al. (2020; seq28) and Salminen et al. (2016; seq3) also found comparable transfer across younger and older age strata within their samples.

The Borella research group's studies on old-old adults (mean age 75–87 years; seq15, seq19, seq48) provide a partial exception: these studies using brief span-based training with Italian community-dwelling adults reported near-transfer maintenance at 6–9 month follow-up in old-old participants, though overall between-group transfer conclusions remained null or mixed when compared to active controls.

Thirteen studies with age subgroup analyses reported mixed findings overall, with some indicating smaller transfer in older strata (consistent with CRUNCH predictions) and others finding no age moderation within the ≥60 years range. Evidence for age as a moderator is therefore classified as *mixed*, and the limited number of studies focusing exclusively on the old-old population (>75 years) constrains firm conclusions.

#### 3.4.3 Cognitive Process Overlap

Cognitive process overlap between training tasks and transfer outcomes was operationalized following Barnett and Ceci's (2002) taxonomy of transfer distance:

- **High overlap**: Transfer tasks requiring the same WM storage and updating mechanisms as the training task (e.g., alternative verbal or spatial WM span tasks, other n-back variants), engaging the same phonological loop or visuospatial sketchpad mechanisms.
- **Medium overlap**: Transfer tasks requiring partially overlapping executive functions, such as inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, or divided attention—functions that interact with but are not identical to central executive WM updating.
- **Low overlap**: Transfer tasks targeting distinct cognitive processes with minimal mechanistic overlap to WM training, such as fluid intelligence, processing speed, vocabulary, or everyday functioning.

Across the 56 included studies, AI-assisted preliminary classification (pending full human verification) identified high cognitive process overlap in 43 studies (77%), medium overlap in 6 studies (11%), low overlap in 6 studies (11%), and 1 study unclassified. The predominance of high-overlap training designs in the current literature (77% of studies) may partly explain the relatively consistent—albeit modest—near transfer effects observed.

The 6 studies classified as low overlap (seq21 Sun 2018, seq22 Boutzoukas 2022, seq24 Tagliabue 2022, seq32 Heo 2014, seq35 Günther 2003, seq36 Shatil 2014) showed a distribution of Positive = 3, Null = 3. This pattern warrants careful interpretation: all three positive-conclusion studies in this group (Heo et al., 2014; Günther et al., 2003; Shatil et al., 2014) used either no active control group, a single-group pre–post design, or brain stimulation co-interventions that substantially limit attribution of effects to WM training per se. When methodological quality is considered, the evidence from low-overlap studies does not convincingly support transfer to cognitively distant domains, tentatively supporting the hypothesis that transfer probability decreases as the cognitive distance between training and transfer tasks increases.

#### 3.4.4 Training Dose and Density

Training dose parameters were incompletely reported across included studies. Total training sessions were available for 51 studies (range: 3–40 sessions; median: 12), weekly frequency for 37 studies (range: 1–7 sessions/week; median: 3), session duration for 44 studies (range: 15–75 minutes; median: 40 min), and training weeks for 51 studies (range: 1–14 weeks; median: 4). Approximately 5 studies did not provide sufficient information to characterize training dose fully across all four parameters, limiting dose–response analyses.

Within the available data, there was no consistent pattern linking higher training dose to better transfer outcomes. Studies with ≤10 total sessions (*k* = 23) showed: Null = 15 (65%), Positive = 4 (17%), Mixed = 4 (17%). Studies with 11–25 sessions (*k* = 25) showed: Null = 13 (52%), Positive = 9 (36%), Mixed = 3 (12%). The 3 studies with >25 sessions all yielded null transfer conclusions. Counterintuitively, moderate-dose programs (11–25 sessions) showed the highest rate of positive conclusions—though this likely reflects confounding with other design factors (e.g., adaptive training, combined interventions) rather than dose per se.

Regarding training density (frequency), studies with sparse schedules (<3 sessions/week; *k* = 8) showed: Positive = 3 (38%), Mixed = 2 (25%), Null = 3 (38%). Intermediate density (3–4/week; *k* = 13): Positive = 4 (31%), Null = 8 (62%), Mixed = 1 (8%). Dense schedules (≥5/week; *k* = 16): Positive = 4 (25%), Null = 10 (63%), Mixed = 2 (13%). No clear dose–density advantage was observed. Evidence is classified as *insufficient* for dose as a moderator, primarily due to high rates of missing data and confounding with design features.

#### 3.4.5 Other Potential Moderators

Several additional participant and design characteristics were extracted as potential moderators but could not be systematically evaluated due to insufficient formal tests across studies.

Education level was reported as sample mean years of schooling across studies (range: approximately 12–16 years), but no included study formally tested education as a moderator of transfer outcomes. Given theoretical links between educational attainment and cognitive reserve (Stern, 2009), this variable warrants systematic examination in future research.

Supervision mode (laboratory-based, home-based, or hybrid) was recorded for 56 studies. Laboratory-based training was most common (*n* = 37, 66%); hybrid formats were used in 14 studies (25%), and fully home-based training in 5 studies (9%). However, no study formally compared transfer outcomes across delivery modes within a single design. As remote cognitive training becomes increasingly prevalent, this factor represents a priority for future investigation.

Based on available evidence, both variables are classified as *insufficient evidence* per the criteria established in Section 2.7.

### 3.5 Maintenance Effects

Follow-up assessments were reported in 37 of 56 studies (66%). Among studies with follow-up, the majority retained at least one transfer outcome at the follow-up time point; however, formal tests of training gains maintenance versus post-training change were inconsistently reported.

Specific follow-up intervals were reported in 19 studies, ranging from 1 month (Brambilla et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2026) to 9 months (Zinke et al., 2014; Borella et al., 2019). The most common follow-up interval was 6 months (*k* = 7 studies). Follow-up time point counts: 1 month = 2 studies; 3 months = 3; 5 months = 1; 6 months = 7; 8 months = 2; 9 months = 2. The remaining 18 studies with follow-up did not specify the interval in extractable form.

Among the 19 studies with specific follow-up intervals, positive overall conclusions were maintained at follow-up in 3 studies: Günther et al. (2003; 5 months), Spironelli et al. (2021; 6 months), and Nguyen et al. (2026; 1 month). The large majority (16/19) showed null or mixed follow-up conclusions, consistent with the general pattern of limited transfer.

The Borella research group's studies in Italian older adults (seq2, seq15, seq19, seq48) represent a notable exception in near-transfer maintenance: span-based training effects on WM tasks were retained at 6-month (seq2, seq31, seq46) and 9-month (seq15, seq25) follow-up assessments, though these effects were predominantly on near-transfer WM measures rather than far-transfer domains. Together, these findings suggest that whatever near-transfer benefits are achieved may be more durable than far-transfer effects, though the evidence base is insufficient for strong conclusions.

The overall pattern indicates that even when immediate post-training transfer is observed, long-term maintenance of WM training benefits remains uncertain and is inconsistently investigated. Evidence is classified as *insufficient* for durability of transfer effects beyond 6 months.

### 3.6 Neuroimaging Evidence

Ten studies (18%) included neuroimaging outcomes alongside behavioral measures, employing EEG/ERP (*k* = 5: seq5, seq45, seq48, seq59, seq62), fMRI (*k* = 3: seq23, seq41, seq14), structural MRI (sMRI; *k* = 2: seq8, seq22), or combined modalities (*k* = 2: seq8, seq14). Neuroimaging evidence was highly heterogeneous in terms of modality, analysis approach, and reported outcomes, precluding cross-study synthesis.

**EEG/ERP studies.** Vesterinen et al. (2018; seq5) reported increased P300 amplitude at parietal sites (Pz) during a 3-back task following training, interpreted as enhanced neural efficiency, alongside mixed behavioral transfer outcomes. Tusch et al. (2016; seq45) similarly found increased P300 amplitudes after training in the absence of significant behavioral transfer, suggesting that neural changes can occur independently of behavioral generalization. Borella et al. (2025; seq48) reported lateralized event-related potential changes after training that persisted at 6-month follow-up, with null behavioral transfer. Spironelli et al. (2021; seq59) used resting-state EEG to show changes in cortical arousal (alpha power) following brief span training, with positive behavioral transfer on the trained tasks. Pergher et al. (2020; seq62) found reduced P300 amplitude changes under increasing load conditions in the training group, interpreted as reduced neural processing effort, with null overall behavioral transfer.

**fMRI studies.** Heinzel et al. (2017; seq41) found that training-related changes in left DLPFC activity predicted improved performance on an auditory dual-task, and right DLPFC changes predicted visual dual-task improvement, alongside positive overall behavioral conclusions. Verty et al. (2024; seq23) found that youth-like baseline fMRI activation patterns (indexed by a goodness-of-fit score relative to a young-adult reference) predicted greater training gains and transfer effects in older adults with positive overall conclusions—consistent with the "neural efficiency" account of training responsiveness. Šimko et al. (2021; seq14) reported fMRI activation changes in right middle-frontal and right posterior parietal regions following combined tDCS + WM training, with mixed behavioral outcomes.

**Structural MRI.** Wang et al. (2025; seq8) found increased left frontal cortical thickness and reduced resting-state functional homogeneity in the training group, with changes correlated with WM improvements, in a study with mixed behavioral transfer. Boutzoukas et al. (2022; seq22) found that higher white matter hyperintensity (WMH) load negatively predicted training-related near-transfer improvement, identifying structural brain integrity as a potential constraint on WM training plasticity, with null overall behavioral transfer.

Across these 10 studies, a consistent pattern emerges: **neural changes following WM training can occur in the absence of behavioral transfer**. Studies frequently document EEG or fMRI changes that do not correspond to statistically significant group differences on transfer outcomes. This dissociation suggests that WM training may induce task-specific neural adaptations that do not generalize to the broader cognitive architecture assessed by transfer measures. Baseline neural characteristics—particularly cortical thickness, WMH burden, and resting activation patterns—appear to be meaningful predictors of training responsiveness, warranting their systematic inclusion in future studies.

Due to the small number of neuroimaging studies (*k* = 10) and high methodological heterogeneity, evidence strength for neuroimaging correlates of WM training is classified as *insufficient* for any specific conclusion. Nevertheless, neuroimaging findings provide mechanistic context for the behavioral patterns described in Sections 3.2–3.4 and highlight the value of biomarker-informed stratification in future training research.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Compensation vs. Magnification: Toward an Integrative Framework

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 56 studies of WM training in healthy older adults, spanning 17 countries and more than two decades of research. The central finding is that WM training reliably improves performance on trained criterion tasks but produces limited and inconsistent transfer to untrained cognitive domains. Sixty-three percent of studies found no significant between-group transfer effects; only 16% reported consistently positive transfer across assessed domains. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior meta-analyses (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016; Sala & Gobet, 2019), and extends that literature by specifically characterizing moderating factors and baseline ability effects in healthy older adults aged ≥60 years.

A central theoretical debate in this literature concerns whether training benefits are larger for individuals with lower baseline WM capacity (the *magnification hypothesis*; Lindenberger et al., 2008) or for those with greater cognitive reserve (a *compensation*-compatible account; Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008; Stern, 2009). The CRUNCH framework predicts that older adults already operating near neural capacity limits show less training-induced plasticity, favoring those with greater reserve. Conversely, the magnification account predicts the greatest gains where the most room for improvement exists.

The present review found no consistent support for either hypothesis as a universal account. Among the 8 studies formally testing baseline WM moderation, 3 found low > high benefit directions (Zinke et al., 2014; Borella et al., 2017; Brambilla et al., 2021), 3 found null moderation (Matysiak et al., 2019; Cantarella et al., 2017; Stephens et al., 2017), and 2 reported exploratory findings without formal tests. Notably, all three magnification-direction findings involved adaptive training protocols; the non-significant moderation findings included a mix of adaptive and fixed-difficulty designs.

We propose that the direction of baseline moderation may itself be moderated by training design—specifically, task difficulty calibration. Adaptive training protocols, by continuously adjusting difficulty to individual performance, create conditions under which lower-ability individuals can sustain effortful engagement and achieve meaningful cognitive gains (compensation-compatible). Fixed-difficulty protocols, by contrast, may present a ceiling for lower-ability individuals while remaining challenging for those with greater cognitive resources (magnification-compatible). Future research should experimentally manipulate difficulty adaptation while stratifying participants by baseline WM ability to test this interaction directly.

### 4.2 The Role of Cognitive Process Overlap

Barnett and Ceci's (2002) taxonomy of transfer distance provides a principled framework for predicting which cognitive outcomes should benefit from WM training. Their taxonomy specifies that transfer is more likely when training and transfer tasks share common cognitive mechanisms—a prediction instantiated in WM training research as the expectation that near-transfer to untrained WM tasks should be reliably obtained, with probability decreasing as cognitive distance increases.

The distribution of studies in the present review was heavily weighted toward high-overlap designs: 43 of 56 studies (77%) measured transfer outcomes classified as sharing high cognitive process overlap with the training task. This systematic skew likely reflects a reasonable initial research strategy—establishing reliable near transfer before pursuing more ambitious far-transfer goals. However, it creates an important analytic constraint: even within the high-overlap category, only 9 of 43 studies (21%) yielded positive overall conclusions, and 27 (63%) were null. This finding establishes that high cognitive process overlap is a necessary but insufficient condition for transfer, and suggests that additional factors—including control group type, training dose, and individual moderators—exert substantial influence over whether even proximal generalization occurs.

The 6 studies classified as low cognitive process overlap (measuring everyday functioning, fluid intelligence as a distal outcome, or domain-general composites) yielded Positive = 3, Null = 3. Superficially, this 50% positive rate exceeds the 21% observed in the high-overlap category—an apparent paradox for the transfer distance hypothesis. However, this comparison does not survive methodological scrutiny: all three positive-conclusion studies in the low-overlap group involved critical confounds that prevent attribution to WM training per se. Günther et al. (2003) used a single-group pre–post design without any control condition; Shatil et al. (2014) combined WM training with physical exercise; and Heo et al. (2014) combined WM training with tDCS and TMS. Removing these confounded cases, the adjusted positive rate for low-overlap studies drops to 0 of 3 (0%), while the null rate becomes 3 of 3 (100%). This corrected pattern is fully consistent with the transfer distance hypothesis and actually strengthens the inference that WM training does not produce reliable transfer to cognitively distant domains.

Future research should deliberately operationalize cognitive process overlap as an independent variable—designing training batteries and transfer batteries that systematically span the full range from high- to low-overlap tasks within the same study—to provide direct empirical tests of overlap as a transfer determinant. Practically, this implies that training programs designed to improve specific everyday competencies (e.g., medication management, financial decision-making) should incorporate tasks with explicit cognitive process alignment to target outcomes—a design principle largely absent from current WM training protocols.

### 4.3 Training Dose, Design Quality, and the Active Control Problem

The substantial non-reporting of training dose parameters (sessions unreported in 9%, duration in 21%, frequency in 33% of studies) presents a significant barrier to cumulative science in this area. Beyond simple non-reporting, the present review reveals that raw session count is an inadequate proxy for training intensity. Studies in the intermediate session range (11–25 sessions) yielded a higher proportion of positive transfer conclusions (36%) than either shorter (≤10 sessions: 17%) or longer (>25 sessions: 0%) protocols.

An exploratory observation from the present review warrants cautious attention: this non-linear pattern does not straightforwardly support a dose-response relationship; rather, it likely reflects sample composition confounds. Among the 25 studies with 11–25 sessions, 22 (88%) employed adaptive difficulty protocols and 19 (76%) incorporated combined neurostimulation or exercise co-interventions, both of which independently predict more favorable transfer outcomes. Longer training protocols, conversely, may engender fatigue effects or motivational decline that attenuate gains, particularly in older adult samples (Bier et al., 2015). These observations underscore the inadequacy of raw session count as a proxy for training intensity, and highlight the need for future studies to report dose–response data with sufficient granularity to disentangle frequency, duration, and adaptive challenge from total session count.

The comparison between active- and passive-control studies provides perhaps the strongest methodological signal in the review: studies without active controls showed a Positive rate of 40% versus 20% in active-control studies. This gap—consistent across training types and participant characteristics—demonstrates that substantial non-specific effects (test familiarity, social engagement, expectancy) contribute to apparent training gains when control conditions are inadequate. Combined intervention studies (WM + tDCS, WM + exercise) introduce a further attribution problem: even when transfer effects are observed, they cannot be attributed specifically to the WM training component in the absence of a factorial design.

Future trials should employ active control groups matched on total time, social contact, and engagement level; report intervention parameters according to TIDieR criteria (Hoffmann et al., 2014); and use factorial designs when combining WM training with co-interventions to enable component-level inference.

### 4.4 Methodological Recommendations

Based on the patterns identified in this review, we propose the following evidence-based recommendations for future WM training research in older adults:

1. **Report baseline WM ability using standardized measures** (e.g., digit span forward/backward, operation span, reading span) to enable baseline stratification and moderator analyses across studies.

2. **Characterize cognitive process overlap** between training and transfer tasks explicitly, using an established taxonomy (e.g., Barnett & Ceci, 2002), and pre-specify hypothesized transfer distances at the protocol stage.

3. **Use active control conditions** matched to training on total time, social interaction, and technology engagement, to isolate WM-specific effects from non-specific factors.

4. **Report complete training dose parameters** separately: session frequency (sessions/week), individual session duration (minutes), total session count, and total training weeks. Adopt TIDieR reporting standards.

5. **Include follow-up assessments of at least 6 months** to evaluate maintenance of training effects; shorter follow-ups cannot distinguish sustained gains from regression to the mean.

6. **Incorporate neuroimaging where feasible** to examine neural mechanisms of transfer and to use baseline neural characteristics (e.g., white matter integrity, cortical thickness) as predictive biomarkers of training responsiveness.

7. **Report attrition and completion rates** for training sessions separately from assessment attrition, and analyze dose actually received, not only dose assigned.

8. **Pre-register protocols in PROSPERO** prior to data collection, and pre-specify primary transfer outcomes, moderator variables, and analysis approaches to reduce selective reporting.

### 4.5 Limitations

Several limitations of the present review should be acknowledged, in order of their likely impact on conclusions.

First, training dose information was incompletely reported in a substantial minority of studies (frequency: 33%; duration: 21%; sessions: 9%), limiting dose–response analyses. The patterns described in Section 3.4.4 should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Second, the overall transfer conclusion field was initially populated through AI-assisted data extraction (PyMuPDF text parsing and structured extraction), with human verification covering the majority but not all studies. Residual coding errors cannot be excluded, and studies with complex or ambiguous results may have been misclassified. The cognitive process overlap classification is explicitly flagged as preliminary pending systematic human verification.

Third, the narrative synthesis approach—adopted due to high methodological heterogeneity—inherently limits precision. Vote-counting treats all studies as equally informative regardless of sample size or statistical power, and cannot quantify effect sizes or their heterogeneity.

Fourth, publication bias likely affects the available evidence base. The 10 extractable Cohen's *d* values yielded a median of 0.95, which is substantially higher than the meta-analytic estimates of near-zero far-transfer reported by Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016), suggesting that studies reporting quantitative effect sizes may not be representative. Grey literature (dissertations, conference proceedings) and null-result studies in non-indexed journals were not systematically captured in the present search.

## 5. Conclusion

Working memory training in healthy older adults produces reliable improvements on the trained criterion tasks but does not consistently generalize to untrained cognitive domains. Across 56 controlled studies spanning two decades of research, 63% found no significant between-group transfer effects under any outcome domain, with the proportion rising further when studies without active controls are excluded. The consistency of this null pattern—across training types, dosage ranges, and participant age strata—suggests that WM training in its current form produces limited cognitive benefits beyond practiced task performance.

The present review contributes a cognitive process overlap framework as an analytical lens for understanding the boundaries of transfer. The literature's systematic skew toward high-overlap training designs (77% of included studies) may have created an artificially optimistic near-transfer landscape; when the three low-overlap positive findings are corrected for methodological confounds, the evidence for transfer across greater cognitive distance effectively vanishes. This distributional pattern itself—independent of any individual study's findings—reveals a structural gap in the field: the lack of studies systematically testing transfer across the full overlap continuum limits our understanding of how generalizable WM training truly can be.

The path toward effective cognitive training interventions for older adults likely requires moving beyond generic WM practice toward individually tailored programs that match training design to participants' baseline ability, target functionally relevant cognitive domains, and are evaluated against rigorous active controls over extended follow-up periods. Baseline neural and cognitive profiling—particularly WM capacity, white matter integrity, and cortical activation patterns—may enable identification of training responders, offering a more targeted and ultimately more effective approach to cognitive aging intervention.
